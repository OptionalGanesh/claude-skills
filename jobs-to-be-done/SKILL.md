# Jobs to Be Done Framework

Framework for discovering innovation based on a fundamental truth: customers don't buy products - they "hire" them to do a specific job in their lives.

## Core Principle

**Job to Be Done** = the progress a customer wants to make in specific circumstances.

Key elements of the definition:
- **Progress** (not goal, not solution) - customer wants to move from current state to a better one
- **Circumstances** - context determines the job, not customer attributes (demographics are useless)
- **Hiring/Firing** - customer actively chooses a product for the "job"

## Scoring

**Goal: 10/10.** When reviewing or creating product strategy or positioning, rate it 0-10 based on adherence to the principles below. A 10/10 means full alignment with all guidelines; lower scores indicate gaps to address. Always provide the current score and specific improvements needed to reach 10/10.

## Three Dimensions of Every Job

Every job has three inseparable dimensions - omitting any means failure:

| Dimension | Question | Example (milkshake) |
|-----------|----------|---------------------|
| **Functional** | What does the customer need to do? | Occupy myself during boring commute |
| **Emotional** | How do they want to feel? | Have a small treat for myself |
| **Social** | How do they want to be perceived? | As a sensible parent (not buying donuts) |

## Framework

### 1. The Job Statement

Format: "When [circumstances], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome]"

- Circumstances matter more than demographics
- A well-written job statement never mentions your product
- Jobs are stable over time; solutions change but the underlying job persists

### 2. Forces of Progress

Four forces determine switching behavior:
- **Push** - frustration with current situation
- **Pull** - attraction of new solution
- **Anxiety** - fear of the new
- **Habit** - comfort with current behavior

Change only happens when Push + Pull > Habit + Anxiety.

### 3. The Big Hire & Little Hire

- **Big Hire**: purchase/signup decision (happens once)
- **Little Hire**: decision to use in the moment (happens repeatedly)

Retention problems are almost always Little Hire failures, not Big Hire failures.

### 4. Competitive Landscape (Non-Obvious Competition)

True competition = everything a customer can "hire" for the same job.
- A milkshake competes with bananas, bagels, boredom, and podcasts
- Non-consumption (doing nothing) is often the biggest competitor
- Map all alternatives including workarounds

### 5. Customer Discovery Interviews

Don't ask "what do you need?" - reconstruct the purchase timeline:
- First thought: "When did you first think about looking for a solution?"
- Search: "What alternatives did you look for?"
- Purchase moment: "What ultimately convinced you?"
- Usage: "What surprised you? What's still missing?"

### 6. Designing for the Job

Every product decision should answer: "Will this help the customer better accomplish their job?"

Replace:
- Customer satisfaction → "did the job get done?" metrics
- NPS → "reasons for hiring and firing"
- Feature usage → "progress on the job"

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Defining jobs too narrowly around your product | Define from customer's perspective, never mention your product |
| Ignoring emotional and social dimensions | Always complete all three dimensions |
| Only increasing Pull | Map all four forces, especially reduce Anxiety and Habit |
| Winning Big Hire but ignoring Little Hire | Track and optimize repeated usage separately |
| Asking customers "what do you want?" | Use timeline-based discovery interviews |
| Defining competition by product category | Map every alternative including non-consumption |
